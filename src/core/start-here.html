Begin with a task you already have

Pick something on your list this week and start. Tell the model what you are trying to accomplish and ask what to do next. Try the suggestion and look at the result. When something fails or confuses you, paste in what happened and ask again.

The first attempt is usually rough, and that is expected. People who get the most from these tools tend to be the ones who keep experimenting and giving feedback, whatever their technical background.

You own the final product

The model can do a large share of the drafting, organizing, and building. The decisions about what is correct and what goes out under your name stay with you.

  • Send back weak output. If a draft is wrong, vague, too formal, or poorly organized, say so and keep revising until it is good.
  • Understand what you share. Before something leaves your hands, be able to explain it and be comfortable signing it.
  • Verify what matters. Check important facts, calculations, sources, and conclusions against their origin, with more care as the stakes rise. Leave out anything you are unable to verify.
  • High-stakes decisions stay with a person. Decisions that affect a patient's care or someone's employment, education, or discipline are made by a qualified person. AI can inform them.
  • Keep sensitive information in approved systems. The privacy and data-governance rules that cover the rest of your work cover this too.

On a KU computer? Gemini and NotebookLM open on KU computers, and they are still personal accounts: public information only, the same as any other personal AI tool.

Working within standard KUMC Copilot. Copilot Chat, the version most people have, keeps a project in a single chat with the files you upload to it. That suits smaller pieces of sensitive work, and the chat's context window limits how large a project can grow. A premium Microsoft 365 Copilot license removes much of that limit by working with your OneDrive files directly. If you are working on a large project that uses only public information and you lack the premium tier, a personal subscription may serve you better. It adds connectors to other apps, access to files on your desktop, and a larger context window. To request the premium tier, contact IT.

A general question or a real patient?

This distinction decides which tool to open for a clinical question.

General question: any tool
Man in his 30s with pleuritic chest pain and an elevated CRP. Give me a differential, ordered by what I should rule out first.

A textbook-style question. It describes a kind of presentation and could apply to thousands of people, so it contains no health system data.

Real patient: @kumc.edu tools only
34-year-old man with Marfan syndrome and an aortic root repair in 2019, here with tearing chest pain since 6 am. Troponin 0.09, D-dimer 2,340, CT report pasted below. What am I missing?

No name, and still one person: an exact age, a rare history, a surgery year, and specific results taken from the chart.

The principle: if the details are enough to point to a specific person, the question stays out of personal AI tools. Identifiability is hard to judge from the inside, because an exact age, an unusual history, and specific results can identify someone without a name.

A practical test is to ask where the details came from. If you could have written the question without ever meeting the patient, it is a general question. If the details come from a chart or an encounter, use a tool you sign in to with your @kumc.edu account, or rewrite the question as a textbook would pose it and leave the encounter out.

Author's readingThe health system policy allows general AI tools "provided that no sensitive, confidential or restricted data is entered." It does not address de-identified cases directly. The reading above is deliberately conservative, since KUMC Copilot is available for real cases. The Data Classification policy and the Compliance team have the final word.
