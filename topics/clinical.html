Topic module

Clinical questions and patient communication

First draftA starting version for the author to revise. The tool list reflects what is named elsewhere in this guide; confirm current availability on the health system's Artificial Intelligence intranet page.

Pocket card

  • For a clinical question, prefer a tool that searches the literature and shows its sources.
  • General questions can go to any tool. Anything about a real patient goes only to tools you sign in to with @kumc.edu.
  • For patient-facing writing, give the reading level, the length, and the action you need the patient to take, then check every instruction yourself.

Looking something up at the point of care

Clinical lookup is the most familiar use of AI in medicine, and the one where an error costs the most. Two choices matter: which tool, and how the question is phrased.

Phrasing a clinical question

  • Ask open questions. Give the findings and ask for a differential or the options. A question that names your leading diagnosis tends to get agreement back; Iterate and verify explains why.
  • Ask for the source and its date. "Which guideline is this from, and what year?" Then open it. Recommendations change after a model's training ends.
  • Verify doses, thresholds, and intervals every time. These are the details a model is most likely to state confidently and get wrong.
  • Use the answer to direct your reading. The tool points you to the relevant guideline or trial faster than a manual search. The clinical decision stays with you and the primary source.
A general question, well phrased
Adult with a chemical splash to the eye, alkali suspected. Summarize current recommendations for irrigation: fluid, volume, duration, and the pH endpoint. Name the guideline or review each point comes from and its year, and tell me where sources disagree.

Writing for patients

Models are good at turning clinical language into plain language, and at producing the same content at several reading levels or in another format. Common uses include discharge instructions, a handout explaining a diagnosis, and return precautions written as a short list.

Give the model four things: the reading level, the length, what the patient needs to do, and when to come back.

Plain-language instructions
Write discharge instructions for an adult with a corneal abrasion. Sixth-grade reading level, under 200 words, short sentences. Sections: what this is, what to do at home, what to avoid, when to come back right away. Use the medication plan I give below and add nothing to it.

A request like the one above describes a condition, so it contains no patient information and any tool may be used. Once you add a specific patient's details, the work belongs in an @kumc.edu tool.

Before it reaches a patient

  • Read every instruction against your own plan. The model may add advice you did not give, or soften a return precaution.
  • Check medication names, doses, and durations against the order.
  • Translations need review by a qualified interpreter or a fluent clinician before use.
  • Follow your department's process for what becomes part of the record.

Where the judgment stays

The health system's policy places final responsibility for decisions that affect a patient's care with a qualified person. In practice: the tool can widen a differential, find the guideline, and draft the handout. Choosing the diagnosis, the treatment, and the words the patient hears is the clinician's work.
