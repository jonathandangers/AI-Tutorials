Module 04

Iterate and verify

04

Pocket card

  • Treat the first output as a draft to react to. Give specific feedback and repeat until it passes your checks.
  • Models lean toward agreeing with you. Ask for the case against.
  • Models can invent references and facts, and their knowledge stops at a training date. Open the source for anything that matters.

The loop

  1. Look closely. Does it answer the question you asked? Is anything missing, wrong, generic, or out of character?
  2. Give specific feedback. "Shorter." "Less formal." "You missed this constraint." "That conclusion does not follow." "Keep A, remove B, reorganize C."
  3. Repeat. Continue until it passes the checks you set for this kind of work.
  4. Change perspective when stuck. Take a break, open a fresh chat from your saved project state, or ask another model to critique the work.

Decide what "done" means

Polished, fluent output makes almost everyone feel finished too early, and the less you know about a topic, the sooner that feeling arrives. Before you sign off, pick two or three things you can confirm.

Kind of workDone when
Meeting summaryEvery decision traces to a moment in the transcript; owners and dates come from the meeting; skipped agenda items are flagged
Paper critiqueNumbers match the paper's tables; claims about design (randomized, blinded) match the methods; associations are reported as associations
Spreadsheet or calculationTwo or three values recomputed separately; totals reconcile
Teaching materialEach recommendation matches the cited guideline and its current version; doses and thresholds checked against the source
Reference listEvery citation opens in PubMed, and the summary matches the abstract

Models tend to agree with you

LLMs are trained to be helpful, and that pulls them toward telling you what you seem to want to hear. Pitch a weak idea with enthusiasm and you will often get enthusiasm back. Push back on a correct answer and the model may give in.

The clinical version is anchoring. Ask "This is costochondritis, right?" about a young man with pleuritic chest pain and you are likely to get a paragraph supporting costochondritis. Ask "What would I regret missing in this presentation?" and the same model raises pulmonary embolism, pericarditis, and pneumothorax. The framing of the question set the answer.

  • Ask for the opposing case. "Argue against this plan as a skeptical program director would."
  • Keep your preference to yourself. "Here are two options. Which is stronger and why?" draws a more honest answer than "I like option A. Is it good?"
  • Ask open questions about a diagnosis. Give the findings and ask for a differential before you offer your leading impression.
  • Notice easy agreement. If the model reversed itself the moment you objected, ask what changed. A specific reason is fine. A reversal with no reason is a warning.
  • Use a second model to find problems. A fresh model can spot issues the first one missed. Two models agreeing is weak evidence, since they share much of their training and can make the same mistake. Confirmation comes from the source.

Models can make things up

A model answering from its training can produce a reference, statistic, dose, or quote that looks right and does not exist. The most common version in medicine is the invented citation: real-sounding authors, a plausible journal, a year, and no such paper.

  • Open every reference. Search the title in PubMed. If you cannot find it, treat it as fabricated.
  • Prefer tools that retrieve. A model searching PubMed or a document you supplied can still misread it, but it starts from something real.
  • Compare the summary with the source. A real paper can still be summarized wrong. Read the abstract or the relevant section yourself.
  • Raise your bar with the stakes. A brainstorm can tolerate errors. Clinical content, publications, and anything sent under your name need every fact checked.

Guidelines change after the model stops learning

Each model is trained on text up to a cutoff date, often many months before you use it. A guideline revised since then, a drug recall, or a new trial may be missing, and the model will present the older recommendation with full confidence. Older guidance is also over-represented, because years of text describe the previous version.

  • Ask which guideline and which year the answer is based on, then open the current version yourself.
  • For anything clinical, use a tool that searches live sources, or paste the current guideline into the chat and ask the model to work from it.
  • Treat doses, thresholds, and screening intervals as items to verify every time.

Know where an answer came from

An answer can come from the model's learned knowledge, from material you supplied, or from a tool that retrieves a live source such as PubMed, a website, or a connected drive. These deserve different levels of trust. For important work, ask which source was used, open the underlying material, and confirm that the model's summary matches it.

Where the answer came fromHow much to trust itWhat to check
The model's learned knowledgeGood for concepts and drafts; unreliable for specific facts; may be out of dateAny number, name, date, guideline, or reference
Material you suppliedGrounded, but can be misreadThat the summary matches the document
A retrieval tool (search, PubMed, connected drive)Grounded in a live sourceThat it found the right source and read it correctly
