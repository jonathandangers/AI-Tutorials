Module 04

Treat the first output as something to react to.

A polished answer is not the same as a correct one. This module covers how to push output from good-looking to trustworthy, and two failure modes to watch for.

04

Pocket card

  • Look closely, give specific feedback, repeat until it passes your checks.
  • Models tend to agree with you. Ask for the case against.
  • Models can invent references and facts. Open the source for anything that matters.

The loop

  1. Look closely. Does it actually answer the question? Is anything missing, wrong, generic, or out of character?
  2. Give specific feedback. "Shorter." "Less formal." "You missed this constraint." "That conclusion does not follow." "Keep A, remove B, reorganize C."
  3. Repeat. Continue until it passes the checks you set for this kind of work, not merely until it looks polished.
  4. Change perspective when stuck. Take a break, open a fresh thread from your saved project state, or ask another model to critique the work.

Decide what "done" means New

Confidence is not a check. Polished, fluent output makes almost everyone feel finished too early, and the less you know about a topic, the sooner that feeling arrives. Before you sign off, pick two or three things you can actually confirm.

Kind of workDone when
Meeting summaryEvery decision traces to a moment in the transcript; no owners or dates were invented; skipped agenda items are flagged
Paper critiqueNumbers match the paper's tables; claims about design (randomized, blinded) match the methods; associations are not stated as causes
Spreadsheet or calculationTwo or three values recomputed separately; totals reconcile
Teaching materialEach recommendation matches the cited guideline and its version; doses and thresholds checked against the source
Reference listEvery citation opens in PubMed, and the summary matches the abstract

Done means it passes your checks, not that it looks polished.

Models tend to agree with you New

LLMs are trained to be helpful, and that pulls them toward telling you what you seem to want to hear. Pitch a weak idea with enthusiasm and you will often get enthusiasm back. Push back on a correct answer and the model may cave.

  • Ask for the opposing case. "Argue against this plan as a skeptical program director would."
  • Hide your preference. "Here are two options. Which is stronger and why?" works better than "I like option A. Is it good?"
  • Notice too-easy agreement. If the model reversed itself the moment you objected, ask it what changed. A real reason is fine; no reason is a warning.
  • Use a second model to find problems, not to confirm. A fresh model can spot issues the first one missed. But two models agreeing is not verification: they share much of their training and can make the same mistakes. Confirmation means checking against the source.

Models can make things up New

A model answering from its training can produce a reference, statistic, dose, or quote that looks right and does not exist. The most common version in medicine is the invented citation: real-sounding authors, a plausible journal, a year, and no such paper.

  • Every reference gets opened. Search the title in PubMed. If you cannot find it, treat it as fabricated.
  • Prefer tools that retrieve. A model searching PubMed or a document you supplied can still misread it, but it starts from something real.
  • Check that the summary matches the source. A real paper can still be summarized wrong. Read the abstract or the relevant section yourself.
  • Raise your bar with the stakes. A brainstorm can tolerate errors. Anything clinical, published, or sent under your name cannot.

Know where an answer came from

An answer can come from the model's learned knowledge, from material you supplied, or from a tool that retrieves a live source such as PubMed, a website, or a connected drive. Those are not equivalent. For important work, ask what source was actually used, open the underlying material when needed, and verify that the model's summary or interpretation matches it.

Where the answer came fromHow much to trust itWhat to check
The model's learned knowledgeGood for concepts and drafts; unreliable for specific factsAny number, name, date, or reference
Material you suppliedGrounded, but can be misreadThat the summary matches the document
A retrieval tool (search, PubMed, connected drive)Grounded in a live sourceThat it found the right source and read it correctly
