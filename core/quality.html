Module 05

Quality, slop, and when to skip AI

05

First draftWritten from the outline for this module. The author's own examples and voice are still to come.

Pocket card

  • Slop is fluent, generic output passed along without your judgment added. It can be entirely accurate.
  • Before you send, cut it down and add what only you know.
  • Some work is better done yourself: when the effort is the point, when checking would take longer than doing, or when no approved tool may see the information.

What slop is

Iterate and verify asks whether the output is correct. This module asks whether it is worth another person's time.

Slop is output that reads smoothly, sounds plausible, and could have been written about any department by anyone. The sender asked for a draft and forwarded it. Every sentence may be true, and the reader still finishes with nothing they can use, because nobody decided what mattered.

The same tool produces the opposite result when you bring your own knowledge to it: work you could not have reached alone, in less time. The difference lies in what you add after the first draft.

How to recognize it

These signs apply to your own drafts and to what lands in your inbox.

  • Length without content. Three paragraphs where two sentences would serve.
  • Hedging on every claim, so the piece never commits to a recommendation.
  • Lists that always come in threes, and paragraphs that all have the same shape.
  • No specifics: no names, numbers, dates, or local detail.
  • Nothing that only the sender could have written.

A side-by-side

Illustrative: an email to faculty about a schedule change.

First draft, forwarded

I hope this message finds you well. I am writing to share some important updates regarding our upcoming simulation sessions. After careful consideration and valuable input from stakeholders, we have made adjustments designed to enhance the learning experience. Please review the attached details at your earliest convenience.

Accurate. The reader still has to open the attachment to learn anything.
After editing

Simulation moves from the first Tuesday to the second Thursday, 1–3 pm, starting in January. The room is unchanged. If you teach on Thursdays, reply by Friday so I can find coverage.

Shorter, specific, and it tells the reader what to do.

Before you send

Match the effort to the audience. A note to yourself needs little review. Anything going to a group deserves a real edit: shorten it, clarify it, and replace general statements with the details you know.

  • Are the sources credible, and did I open them?
  • What is missing that I know and the model did not?
  • Could this be half as long?
  • Would I sign this, and would I say it out loud on rounds?

When to skip AI

Four situations where doing the work yourself is the better choice.

  • The effort is the point. When you are learning a skill or forming your own judgment, a finished answer from the model skips the part that teaches you. Either do the task unaided or switch to practice mode, where the model coaches and you do the thinking.
  • Checking would take longer than doing. A short note you can write in two minutes rarely benefits from a draft you then have to verify line by line.
  • No approved tool may see the information. Some material belongs in none of the tools available to you. The data rules decide this before convenience does.
  • The reader expects your own words. A condolence note, feedback to a struggling learner, or a difficult conversation with a patient carries weight because it comes from you. Using the model to think through what you want to say is reasonable. Sending its text is a different act, and the recipient may trust the message less if they learn of it.
