Module 02

Ways to use an LLM

02

Pocket card

  • Six roles: thinking partner, tutor, organizer, writer, research assistant, builder.
  • Tell the model whether you want a finished product or want to practice.
  • Dictate the messy version first and let the model organize it.
  • Ask for the output you need: a teaching page, a handout, a table, a quiz.

Six ways to use an LLM

  • Thinking and strategy partner

    Brainstorm, pressure-test an idea, compare approaches, or look for blind spots in a plan before you commit to it.

  • Tutor and guide

    Ask it to teach you something new, or to guide you through a task while you do it. Keep asking follow-up questions until the unclear parts make sense.

  • Organizer for messy thinking

    Brain-dump by typing or voice. The model turns it into an outline, plan, email, or table, and you react and revise.

  • Writer and editor

    Draft, shorten, restructure, or adapt a piece for a particular audience. You decide what is worth saying and whether it sounds like you.

  • Research and analysis assistant

    Search connected sources, synthesize papers, extract data, and critique reasoning.

  • Builder and operator

    Create files, databases, presentations, websites, and small apps. With the right connections, it can also work inside the systems where your material lives.

Production or practice?

Decide at the start of a session which kind of work you are doing, and tell the model. Models default to production: they hand over answers unless you ask for coaching.

Production

You want a finished product you would sign. The model does much of the drafting. You shape it, push back, and check it against your standards before it goes anywhere. Iterate and verify covers what checking involves.

Practice

You want to get better at the task itself. You do the work and the model coaches: it asks questions, gives hints, and critiques your attempt. Choose this when the effort is what builds the skill.

Practice mode is the larger opportunity for learners

In practice mode you have a very bright instructor who is available at any hour, never tires, and is willing to help with anything you bring. It can give feedback on everything you write, produce as many examples of a concept as you need, and build a quiz on any topic at the level you choose. That is a different use from "please do this for me," and for a resident or student it is usually the more valuable one.

Some tools include a study or learning mode that sets this up for you. In any tool, a few sentences are enough:

Oral-board drill
Act as an oral boards examiner in emergency medicine. Give me one case at a time. Reveal findings only when I ask for them. Hold your feedback until I commit to a diagnosis and plan, then tell me what I missed and what an examiner would have wanted to hear.
Feedback on my own attempt
I will write my assessment and plan first. Then tell me what is missing or weak, and ask me two questions that test whether I understand why. Leave the rewriting to me.
More examples, then a quiz
Explain acid-base compensation with three worked examples of increasing difficulty. Then quiz me with five questions, one at a time, and give a hint before the answer.

Brain-dump first, organize second

This is one of the highest-value habits. Type or dictate the messy version of what you are thinking, then ask the model to organize it. React to what comes back: "keep this," "move that," "this is too formal," "I forgot something." The model handles the writing and reorganizing while you supply the ideas.

Voice works well here. Most apps have a microphone button, and two minutes of talking produces more context than most people would type.

What you can ask it to make

A chat reply is one kind of output. The same request can end in a file you can open, share, or publish.

KindExamples from academic medicine
Teaching pagesA visual HTML page built from your Word lecture notes; an interactive case; a topic hub that links several lectures
HandoutsA one-page reference for a rotation, a pocket card, a plain-language patient handout
SlidesA slide outline with speaker notes, built from a chapter or your own outline
QuizzesBoard-style questions with explanations, written from material you supply
Structured tablesA comparison of guidelines, a summary of papers, a curriculum map
Planning documentsA project description, a timeline, an agenda, a meeting summary
Working toolsA tracking database, a small website, a scheduled literature search

Converting material from one format to another is a strong use. A dense Word document can become a page with headings, diagrams, and a quiz, and the content stays yours.
